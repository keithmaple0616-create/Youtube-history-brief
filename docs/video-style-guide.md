# Video Style Guide

## Channel Video Identity

The channel should feel like a calm, intelligent documentary essay for North American viewers. It should be visually serious enough for politics and history, but still paced for YouTube.

Working style:

> Archival documentary essay with modern explanatory graphics.

The viewer should feel they are watching a thoughtful video, not reading a lecture deck.

## Visual Mix

Use four kinds of visuals:

1. Real material
   - Archival photos.
   - Government footage.
   - News screenshots.
   - Maps.
   - Documents.
   - Public speeches, buildings, crowds, borders, institutions.

2. Editorial motion
   - Slow zooms.
   - Slow pans.
   - Crops that guide the eye.
   - Split screens.
   - Document reveals.
   - Timeline moves.

3. Explainer graphics
   - Maps.
   - Relationship diagrams.
   - Timelines.
   - Cause-and-effect chains.
   - "Similar / different" comparisons.

4. Minimal title cards
   - Section titles.
   - Short thesis statements.
   - Dates and place labels.

Title cards should support the video. They should not become the video.

## Real Footage Rule

The channel should prioritize real moving footage whenever possible.

For a 60-90 second sample:

- At least 40-60% of screen time should use real moving footage, official video, archival video, or documentary-style source footage.
- Still images may support the argument, but they should not dominate the whole sample.
- If a sample uses only still images, it should be treated as a mood board, not as a valid video test.

For a full weekly episode:

- Every major section should include some moving footage or source-grounded visual material unless the topic genuinely lacks usable footage.
- A section made only of title cards, static diagrams, and Ken Burns photos should be flagged as high PPT risk.
- Maps and diagrams should explain structure after the viewer has already seen concrete people, places, documents, or events.

## Pacing Rules

For a finished YouTube video:

- Change the visual idea every 4-8 seconds.
- Use longer holds only when the voiceover is emotionally or intellectually important.
- Avoid more than 12 seconds of a static graphic unless details are actively changing.
- Use motion to reveal meaning, not just to decorate.
- Keep subtitles readable and stable.

For a 60-90 second sample:

- 8-12 visual beats is a good target.
- At least half of the beats should include real or realistic source material.
- Explainer graphics should appear after the viewer understands the concrete event.

## Opening Minute Formula

A strong opening minute should usually follow this pattern:

1. A vivid current image or news hook.
2. A historical echo.
3. The tempting but misleading comparison.
4. The real structure underneath.
5. The promise of the episode.

For the Nixon/Trump China sample:

```text
Current image:
An American president lands in Beijing.

Historical echo:
Nixon in 1972.

Misleading comparison:
It looks like Nixon 2.0.

Real structure:
Nixon had the Soviet triangle. Trump does not.

Episode promise:
The photo rhymes, but the strategic music is different.
```

## Recommended Shot Types

Use these repeatedly across the channel:

- Archival photo push-in.
- Newspaper headline sweep.
- Map line animation.
- Document close-up.
- Split-screen comparison.
- Speaker at podium.
- Institutional exterior.
- Timeline marker.
- Source card.
- Lower-third date/place label.
- Diagram over footage.

## Typography

Use restrained typography:

- Headings: serif, editorial, confident.
- Captions and labels: clean sans-serif.
- Source/date labels: monospace or compact sans-serif.

Avoid:

- Oversized text covering most of the frame too often.
- Meme-style kinetic text.
- Long paragraphs on screen.
- Text that repeats the voiceover word for word for too long.

## Color And Texture

Base palette:

- Dark ink backgrounds.
- Warm archival paper.
- Muted red for China, warning, or tension.
- Cold blue for U.S., institutions, or maps.
- Soft off-white for text.

Texture:

- Light film grain.
- Paper texture.
- Subtle scan lines only when they support archival mood.

Avoid:

- Neon colors.
- Decorative gradient blobs.
- Overly glossy tech graphics.
- Fake patriotic spectacle.

## Sound Design

The video should not rely on voiceover alone.

Use:

- Low, restrained background music.
- Soft whooshes for transitions.
- Paper/document movement sounds.
- Subtle archive or projector texture.
- Very light impact sounds for important diagram moments.

Avoid:

- Loud trailer booms.
- Distracting music.
- Comedy sound effects.
- Constant transition noises.

## Voiceover

Temporary AI voiceover is acceptable for drafts.

Final target:

- Calm.
- Literate.
- Slightly dry.
- Not announcer-like.
- Not overly dramatic.
- Clear pronunciation of Chinese names and historical terms.

The voice should make the viewer feel guided, not sold to.

## Use Of AI Images

Allowed:

- Abstract supporting illustrations.
- Non-specific mood images.
- Maps and diagrams.
- Clearly stylized explanatory visuals.

Not allowed:

- Fake historical photos presented as real.
- Fake images of real political figures in events that did not happen.
- AI-generated "news footage" that could mislead viewers.

If a visual is illustrative, the production notes should treat it as illustration, not evidence.

## Caption Style

Captions should be used selectively:

- Key thesis lines.
- Names and dates.
- Places.
- Short quotes.
- Complex terms.

Avoid full karaoke subtitles for the whole video unless a platform-specific cut requires them.

## Nixon/Trump V3 Learning

The third quick draft improved because it added official White House moving footage to the opening. This confirmed an important production rule:

> Real video changes the perceived category of the work. Still images with motion feel like a presentation; real footage plus restrained graphics starts to feel like YouTube documentary.

Future samples should begin with moving footage whenever possible, then use stills and graphics as supporting layers.

## Quality Bar

A sample is good enough to continue if:

- It still makes sense with the sound off.
- It still feels watchable with temporary voiceover.
- A viewer can tell what kind of channel this is within 10 seconds.
- The visuals create curiosity before the argument becomes complex.
- The explainer graphics clarify something that footage alone cannot show.
